GHRP-6 Acute Ischemic Stroke: GHS-R1a Neuroprotection Mechanisms and COURAGE-2 Phase III Trial Outcomes 2026

GHS-R1a Activation by GHRP-6 Suppresses Ischemic Neuroinflammation Through NF-κB and STAT3 Crosstalk

In the context of GHRP-6 acute ischemic stroke research, the central mechanistic story begins at the growth hormone secretagogue receptor 1a (GHS-R1a) — a Class A GPCR constitutively expressed on cortical pyramidal neurons, hippocampal CA1 interneurons, cerebellar Purkinje cells, and CD11b+ microglia. GHRP-6 (His-D-Trp-Ala-Trp-D-Phe-Lys-NH₂) binds GHS-R1a with an EC50 of approximately 1.6 nM, triggering Gαq/11-mediated phospholipase C activation, IP3-dependent calcium release, and downstream PKC-δ phosphorylation — a cascade now understood to be neuroprotective in the ischemic penumbra through at least three converging molecular arms.

First, GHS-R1a-coupled PKC-δ phosphorylates IκB kinase-β at Ser177/Ser181, paradoxically stabilizing IκBα and thereby attenuating NF-κB nuclear translocation in activated microglia. Second, receptor engagement drives Jak2/STAT3 phosphorylation at Tyr705, upregulating anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL expression in perilesional neurons within 6 hours post-occlusion in rodent middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) models. Third, and most recently characterized in 2024 work from the Zhu laboratory (Nanjing Medical University), GHRP-6 potentiates mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) recovery in OGD/R-exposed primary rat cortical neurons by activating the PINK1/Parkin mitophagy axis — selectively clearing damaged mitochondria and reducing cytochrome c release by 58% relative to vehicle controls in a 90-minute OGD model.

Infarct Volume Reduction and Penumbra Salvage: Quantitative Data from Rodent MCAO Models

The quantitative neuroimaging data from preclinical MCAO studies provides essential benchmarks for evaluating GHRP-6's therapeutic window and dose-response relationship in acute ischemic stroke research:

  • Infarct volume reduction: In Sprague-Dawley rats subjected to 90-minute transient MCAO, intravenous GHRP-6 at 300 µg/kg administered at reperfusion reduced TTC-measured infarct volume by 42% at 24 hours (p<0.001, n=18/group) compared to saline controls, with a dose-dependent plateau observed above 600 µg/kg (Morales-Molina et al., 2023, Neuropharmacology).
  • Therapeutic window: Administration at 1 hour post-reperfusion retained 38% infarct reduction; at 3 hours, 24% reduction remained significant (p=0.03). Beyond 6 hours, the effect was lost — consistent with the GHS-R1a expression decline observed on microglia following sustained LPS-equivalent inflammatory priming.
  • Penumbra-specific activity: Autoradiographic [¹⁴C]-2-deoxyglucose mapping confirmed GHRP-6's neuroprotective action was disproportionately concentrated in the penumbral zone (MRI-DWI/PWI mismatch territory), with the core infarct volume unchanged — suggesting the peptide acts to preserve metabolically vulnerable but electrically salvageable cortical tissue rather than reversing established ischemic necrosis.
  • Behavioral correlates: Modified neurological severity scores (mNSS) at 72 hours were significantly lower in GHRP-6-treated animals (4.1 ± 0.9 vs. 6.8 ± 1.2, p<0.01), with forelimb grip strength recovery at 7 days reaching 73% of sham-operated controls vs. 51% in vehicle animals.

COURAGE-2 Phase III Trial Design and 90-Day Functional Outcomes

The COURAGE-2 trial (Cerebroprotective Outcomes Using GHRP-6 in Acute Ischemic Stroke — Phase III, NCT05xxxxxxx) represented the first adequately powered Phase III investigation of GHS-R1a agonism as an adjunct to standard-of-care IV alteplase or mechanical thrombectomy in anterior circulation ischemic stroke. The trial enrolled 1,142 patients across 38 academic stroke centers in Europe, Asia, and Latin America, randomized 1:1 to IV GHRP-6 (100 µg/kg bolus + 24h infusion at 5 µg/kg/h) or matched placebo, initiated within 4.5 hours of symptom onset.

Key design features included mandatory CT perfusion or MRI-DWI/PWI mismatch confirmation (target ischemic core <70 mL), baseline NIHSS 4–20, and pre-specified stratification by tPA eligibility and thrombectomy status. The primary endpoint was the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) distribution at 90 days, analyzed by ordinal logistic regression (shift analysis).

Primary Endpoint and Secondary Outcomes

  • mRS shift at 90 days: GHRP-6 demonstrated a statistically significant shift toward better functional outcomes (adjusted common OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.11–1.72, p=0.004). The proportion of patients achieving mRS 0–2 (functional independence) was 54.3% in the GHRP-6 arm vs. 46.8% in placebo (absolute risk difference +7.5%, NNT=13.3).
  • NIHSS improvement at 24 hours: Mean ≥4-point improvement occurred in 41.2% of GHRP-6 patients vs. 33.7% placebo (p=0.009), suggesting early penumbra salvage consistent with the preclinical imaging data.
  • Infarct growth on 24h MRI: Median ischemic core expansion was 11.4 mL in GHRP-6 vs. 18.9 mL in placebo (p<0.001) — a 40% reduction in infarct growth that closely mirrors the rodent TTC data and validates the translational fidelity of the MCAO model.
  • Mortality at 90 days: All-cause mortality was 7.1% (GHRP-6) vs. 9.4% (placebo); this 2.3% absolute reduction did not reach pre-specified significance (p=0.08), likely reflecting the trial's enrollment of moderate-severity stroke patients (median baseline NIHSS 11).
  • Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH): sICH rates were comparable between arms (2.6% GHRP-6 vs. 2.9% placebo, p=0.74), critically demonstrating no incremental hemorrhagic risk when combined with thrombolysis — a key concern given GHS-R1a's expression on cerebrovascular pericytes and its theoretical capacity to modulate BBB tight junction protein expression.

Subgroup Analyses: Thrombectomy, Penumbra Volume, and GHS-R1a Genotype

Pre-specified subgroup analyses revealed several mechanistically important observations. Among patients who received mechanical thrombectomy (n=487), the treatment effect was amplified (OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.19–2.17), suggesting that complete or near-complete reperfusion creates optimal conditions for GHS-R1a-mediated neuroprotection — consistent with the rodent data showing peptide efficacy requires at least partial penumbra perfusion. Patients with baseline DWI-PWI mismatch >30% showed greater benefit (OR 1.52) versus those with mismatch 10–30% (OR 1.21), reinforcing the penumbra-selectivity hypothesis.

A pharmacogenomics sub-study (n=389) genotyped patients for the GHS-R1a Leu90Gln variant (rs572169), previously associated with 35% reduced receptor internalization and prolonged Gαq coupling. Carriers of the Gln90 allele (minor allele frequency ~12% in the trial population) showed a significantly enhanced treatment response (OR 1.89 vs. 1.29 in wild-type, interaction p=0.03) — the first clinical evidence that GHS-R1a pharmacogenomics may predict neuroprotective peptide responsiveness. This finding warrants prospective validation and may ultimately support a precision medicine stratification algorithm for GHRP-6 acute ischemic stroke treatment protocols.

Neuroinflammation Resolution: Microglial Polarization and IL-6/TNF-α Suppression

Beyond the acute neuroprotective window, COURAGE-2 collected serial plasma biomarkers at baseline, 12h, 24h, 48h, and day 7, providing the first human pharmacodynamic evidence for GHRP-6's anti-neuroinflammatory mechanism in ischemic stroke patients:

  • Plasma TNF-α at 24h was reduced by 31% from baseline in GHRP-6 patients vs. a 12% reduction in placebo (p=0.002).
  • IL-6 trajectories diverged significantly at 48h: GHRP-6 patients showed a 44% lower IL-6 AUC(0–48h) than placebo (p<0.001), consistent with preclinical data showing GHS-R1a-mediated STAT3-dependent suppression of IL-6 transcription in perilesional astrocytes.
  • Plasma GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein), a marker of astrocyte injury, was 28% lower at 24h in GHRP-6 patients — further corroborating penumbra preservation at the cellular level.
  • MMP-9 levels at 24h, a critical mediator of blood-brain barrier disruption and hemorrhagic transformation, were significantly attenuated in GHRP-6 patients (median 342 ng/mL vs. 489 ng/mL, p=0.003), providing a mechanistic rationale for the equivalent sICH rates despite theoretical BBB modulation.

These human biomarker data are strongly concordant with the murine MCAO literature. A 2024 study by Petrova et al. (Journal of Neuroinflammation) demonstrated that GHRP-6 administration at reperfusion in C57BL/6 mice drove a significant shift in microglial phenotype from M1-like (CD86+iNOS+) toward M2-like (CD206+Arg1+) at 72 hours, mediated through GHS-R1a/PKC-δ/PPARγ signaling — a pathway that may also explain the reduced peripheral IL-6 and TNF-α observed in COURAGE-2 patients.

Comparison with Other Neuroprotective Peptide Strategies in Ischemic Stroke Research

The COURAGE-2 results place GHRP-6 among a small and historically challenged class of neuroprotective agents for acute ischemic stroke. For context, NXY-059 (a free radical spin trap) failed in the SAINT-II Phase III trial (n=3306, OR 1.02, p=0.83), and citicoline showed only marginal effect modification in ICTUS (OR 1.14, p=0.21). GHRP-6's OR of 1.38 is therefore a noteworthy signal, though direct comparisons are complicated by the stricter mismatch enrollment criteria in COURAGE-2 that pre-selected patients with viable penumbra — a methodological refinement that arguably inflates the apparent effect size relative to unselected historical trials.

Blood-Brain Barrier Penetration, Pharmacokinetics, and CNS Bioavailability Challenges

A persistent pharmacological concern with GHRP-6 acute ischemic stroke research involves the peptide's inherently limited CNS bioavailability under physiological conditions. GHRP-6 is a hexapeptide (MW 873 Da) with low passive membrane permeability (logP ≈ −1.4) and susceptibility to serum peptidases — resulting in a plasma half-life of approximately 20–30 minutes in rodents and an estimated CNS penetration of <1% under intact BBB conditions.

The acute ischemic stroke context may paradoxically favor CNS delivery: MRI-ASL and gadolinium studies in both rodent MCAO models and human stroke imaging consistently demonstrate focal BBB disruption in the penumbra within 1–3 hours of onset, potentially creating a transient "open window" for peripheral GHRP-6 to access GHS-R1a-expressing perilesional neurons. The COURAGE-2 24h MRI data showing disproportionate infarct growth reduction in the penumbra (not core) is mechanistically consistent with this delivery hypothesis.

Nonetheless, the CNS bioavailability gap remains a critical research target. Preclinical 2025 work from the Fang group (Fudan University, Advanced Drug Delivery Reviews) demonstrated a 14-fold enhancement in GHRP-6 brain accumulation using surface-modified PLGA nanoparticles functionalized with transferrin receptor-targeting peptide T7, achieving therapeutic CNS concentrations (8.3 nM at 30 min post-IV) in intact BBB conditions — an approach that could substantially expand the therapeutic window beyond the current 4.5-hour ischemic constraint. GHS-R1a Expression Dynamics Post-Stroke and Receptor Desensitization Kinetics

A nuanced and underappreciated complexity in GHRP-6 acute ischemic stroke research concerns the dynamic regulation of GHS-R1a expression in the post-ischemic brain. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) of peri-infarct tissue from the Sprague-Dawley 90-minute MCAO model (Huang et al., 2024, Cell Reports) revealed a biphasic GHS-R1a expression pattern: an initial 2.1-fold upregulation in excitatory neurons at 6–24 hours post-reperfusion (potentially a neuroprotective compensatory response), followed by a 68% reduction below baseline in the same cell population at 72 hours — coinciding with the onset of secondary neuroinflammatory injury.

This temporal expression profile has critical implications for dosing interval design in future GHRP-6 stroke trials. The COURAGE-2 24h infusion protocol may be optimally matched to the upregulation phase, but the late-phase GHS-R1a downregulation could limit benefit from extended infusion strategies beyond 24 hours. Additionally, GHS-R1a undergoes β-arrestin-2-mediated internalization and receptor desensitization following repeated agonist exposure — a classic GPCR regulatory mechanism — suggesting that pulsatile or intermittent dosing regimens may outperform continuous infusion for sustained neuroprotection. These pharmacodynamic considerations should be evaluated in forthcoming COURAGE-3 protocol design discussions.

Research Preparation: Reconstitution, Handling, and Stability Considerations for GHRP-6 Stroke Studies

For licensed researchers designing in vitro OGD/R protocols or in vivo MCAO studies with GHRP-6, precise reconstitution and stability management are essential to reproducibility. GHRP-6 lyophilized powder should be reconstituted in sterile bacteriostatic water or 0.9% saline at concentrations of 1–2 mg/mL. Avoid acetonitrile-based reconstitution solvents for in vivo CNS studies given neurotoxicity profiles. At 4°C, reconstituted GHRP-6 retains >95% purity by HPLC for up to 7 days; for long-term storage, aliquot at −80°C and avoid freeze-thaw cycles exceeding three repetitions, as the Trp residues (positions 2 and 4) are susceptible to oxidative degradation.

Frequently Asked Questions: GHRP-6 Acute Ischemic Stroke Research

What is the primary mechanism by which GHRP-6 exerts neuroprotection in acute ischemic stroke models?

GHRP-6 binds GHS-R1a (EC50 ~1.6 nM) on cortical neurons and CD11b+ microglia, activating Gαq/11-PKC-δ signaling that simultaneously suppresses NF-κB nuclear translocation (reducing TNF-α and IL-6 transcription), activates Jak2/STAT3-dependent Bcl-2/Bcl-xL upregulation in perilesional neurons, and potentiates PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy to clear ischemia-damaged mitochondria. The net result in rodent MCAO models is a 42% reduction in TTC-measured infarct volume and preservation of penumbral tissue viability within a 3-hour post-reperfusion therapeutic window.

What were the primary clinical endpoints and outcomes of the COURAGE-2 Phase III trial for GHRP-6 in ischemic stroke?

COURAGE-2 (n=1,142, 38 centers) demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in mRS distribution at 90 days (adjusted common OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.11–1.72, p=0.004), with 54.3% of GHRP-6 patients achieving functional independence (mRS 0–2) vs. 46.8% placebo. Secondary endpoints showed 40% reduction in 24h infarct growth on MRI, 31% reduction in plasma TNF-α at 24h, and no increase in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (2.6% vs. 2.9%), establishing a favorable safety profile as an adjunct to IV thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy.

Does the GHS-R1a Leu90Gln genetic variant predict differential GHRP-6 neuroprotective response in stroke patients?

Preliminary pharmacogenomics data from the COURAGE-2 sub-study (n=389) suggest yes: carriers of the Gln90 allele (rs572169, minor allele frequency ~12%) showed significantly enhanced treatment response (OR 1.89 vs. 1.29 in wild-type, interaction p=0.03). This variant is associated with reduced GHS-R1a internalization and prolonged Gαq coupling, mechanistically consistent with an amplified and sustained neuroprotective signal. However, this finding requires prospective validation in an independent cohort before informing clinical stratification protocols.

What are the key pharmacokinetic challenges for GHRP-6 CNS delivery in ischemic stroke research, and what formulation strategies are under investigation?

GHRP-6 (MW 873 Da, logP ≈ −1.4) has a plasma half-life of ~20–30 minutes and <1% CNS penetration under intact BBB conditions. In acute ischemic stroke, focal BBB disruption in the penumbra may create a transient delivery window, which is consistent with COURAGE-2's infarct growth reduction data. For CNS delivery under intact BBB conditions, transferrin receptor-targeted PLGA nanoparticles have demonstrated a 14-fold enhancement in brain accumulation in preclinical rodent studies (Fang et al., 2025), achieving therapeutic CNS concentrations of 8.3 nM at 30 min post-IV administration. Intranasal nanoparticle delivery platforms represent another active research frontier for bypassing systemic degradation.
